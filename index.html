How to read this

A space jellyfish is a rocket's exhaust plume lit by the Sun while the sky around the observer is dark. This page works out the geometry for each launch: where the rocket is, where the Sun is, and whether the plume is in sunlight while Charleston is not.

  • Likely: every flight path we try has at least a minute of sunlit rocket above 5° in a dark sky. Possible: only some paths do. No: none do.
  • Prime: the Sun is at least 6° below the horizon, so the sky is properly dark.
  • The sky chart shows where to look. Your fist at arm's length covers roughly 10° of sky.
  • The camera button on each launch draws the path over your phone's camera view, so you can see which part your trees hide. Set it up the day before with the Sun; the steps are on that page.
  • The forecast is worked out for James Island, but it holds anywhere from Folly Beach to Isle of Palms: heights and directions usually move by 1 to 2°.

The verdict is geometry only. Cloud is shown separately from the National Weather Service forecast, within about 7 days of launch, and does not change the verdict. The flight paths come from ten Falcon 9 flights in 2018 and 2020. The model rated all 4 earlier plume sightings reported in the Charleston area as likely or possible, and on 13 October 2026 SpX-35 showed a plume from James Island at the predicted time and height. Five sightings are still too few to call it proven. A launch that slips changes everything: check the time again before you go outside.
